## What it does

`hit-products` helps you decide what to build and make it something people keep using. It follows the product-making method Mark Pincus describes in *Life at the Speed of Play* and in [his Lenny's Podcast interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7eh9C3TUotc). The skill is an independent distillation in its own words.

The method rests on one asymmetry: a good founder's **instinct** (the human need they sense) is usually right, but the specific **idea** built on it is usually wrong. So the skill:

- states the instinct separately from your idea, and generates several other ideas on the same instinct;
- breaks each idea into **Proven** (what to copy from products this audience already loves), **Better** (improvements every existing user would agree with) and **New** (the risky novel part, to be tested on its own);
- grades each idea honestly (A, B+, C+...) and says when your original idea isn't the strongest;
- replaces the MVP with the cheapest test that can give real signal, with success and kill thresholds set before it runs;
- designs the core loop, a leading metric specific to your product, retention out to a year, small "Bold Beat" experiments, and how the product spreads.

New ideas come out as a **Hit Plan**, and existing products as an **Engagement Audit**. Either way you also get a short chat summary: the instinct, the recommended bet and its grade, and the test to run this week.

## When to reach for it

You don't have to name it. It's meant to trigger on its own when you:

- have a product, app, startup or feature idea and ask whether it's worth building, or what to build first;
- want to validate, pivot or kill an idea;
- are planning an MVP or launch;
- want to know why a competitor or hit product works (it does a teardown);
- have a live product with an engagement, retention or growth problem ("make it stickier");
- are building on a new platform like AI agents, where little is proven yet;
- run a product team and want a weekly roadmap discipline.

It works for consumer, prosumer, AI and B2B products. It is not for naming (use [business-naming](../branding/business-naming.md)), visual design or writing code.

## It's working if

- The grade of your idea surprises you a little, and the reason is specific.
- You get more than one idea to test, and the test for the first one costs days and little money, not a summer of building.
- Every test has a number that means "this is working" and a number that means "stop".
- Items labelled Better really would get a yes from every existing user. Anything doubtful has been moved to New.
- Competitors are named, with sources and dates, and unverified claims are marked.
- The plan answers "why would someone still use this in a year?"

## Common questions

**Is it just going to tell me my idea is bad?**
No. It separates the instinct from the idea, and usually the instinct holds up. What it questions is the specific version you've fallen for, and it hands you several alternatives plus a cheap way to find out which one has real pull.

**Why not just build an MVP?**
"Viable" usually means months of building before any real signal. The skill tests the riskiest part first with something much smaller: a fake button, an ad, a mock-up stitched from existing products, or doing the service by hand. Once real users show heat, it recommends building properly and launching big.

**Can it tell me whether my idea will work?**
No, and it says so. Only real user behavior can. What it can do is make the bet as strong as possible and design the fastest honest test.

**Why does it take longer than a normal answer?**
It researches the products your idea builds on and current competitors, and reads several reference files. In testing, runs took about three times longer than a plain answer, and produced plans with real competitors, isolated tests and kill rules.

**Does it only work for games?**
No. Many of the examples come from Zynga, but the method has been applied to messaging apps, marketplaces, enterprise chat, TV and retail promotions.

## Where it fits
