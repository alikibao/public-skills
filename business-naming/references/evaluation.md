# Evaluation rubric

Used by the Judge (shortlist and finals) and by the lead when a user brings their own
candidates. Judge in context where possible — a name read in a headline is a different
name from the same word in a spreadsheet.

## Criteria (score each 1–10; never 7)

| Criterion | The question to ask |
|---|---|
| **Original** | Is it new *in this category*? Does it avoid the landscape's not-going-there bucket? (Original ≠ coined: Feather is original in fiber.) |
| **Fluency** | Can you say it at first sight, spell it after hearing it once, and remember it tomorrow? Is there something inside it to grasp? |
| **Surprise** | Is it unexpected in this category *and from this company*? Does it make you look twice? |
| **Believability** | In context, in the first split second, do you lean toward believing it belongs to this business? |
| **Strategy** | Does it do the framework's jobs — say, or open the door to saying, what we need to say? |
| **Legs** | Can it carry a descriptor, a story, a product family, a new market? Could it become a verb? Will it age well? |

**Gut score:** one number 1–10, from the first honest reaction, **no sevens**. A seven
is a way of not deciding; force yourself to 6 or 8.

Don't average the criteria into the gut score. A name with one 4 and a 10 in surprise
can be the right name; a name with six 7-ish scores is usually invisible.

## Zones (the comfort trap)

Plot familiarity (how easily the brain processes it) against distinctiveness (how much
it stands out in the category):

| | Low distinctiveness | High distinctiveness |
|---|---|---|
| **High familiarity** | **Invisible** — safe, consensus, forgettable (Ready Mop, Fiber One) | **Tension** — surprisingly familiar; splits the room; the target (Swiffer, BlackBerry, Feather) |
| **Low familiarity** | Dead — skip | **Noise** — distinctive but nothing to grasp; hard to say, spell or place |

Most of a shortlist should sit in tension. Coined names can live in tension when they
feel familiar (Pentium, Sonos, Febreze sound like they already mean something).

## Red flags

- Descriptive in a category full of descriptive names.
- Fails hear-it-spell-it (the Codium problem).
- Search-hostile: a common word with no category twist and thousands of unrelated
  users, so customers can't find you. (Real words in new contexts are fine when the
  category makes them ownable.)
- Negative, rude or unlucky meaning in a target market.
- Collides with a famous mark, or with anyone in the same or neighbouring goods/services.
- Trend morphemes that date fast (-ify, -ly, dropped vowels, gratuitous .ai styling).
- Only works with an explanation.

## Not red flags

- "I don't like it" on first read — record it, then look for what's underneath.
- A split reaction — that's energy.
- Not describing the product — the descriptor can do that job.
- Unfamiliar at first sight but fluent once heard.

## Spread checklist for any shortlist or final set

- At least three name types (real word in new context, compound, coined, claim…).
- Ideas from more than one territory and more than one team.
- At least two tension names that will polarize the client.
- One option a cautious stakeholder could live with — clearly labelled as such.
