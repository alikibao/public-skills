# The method — principles behind every phase

Distilled (in our own words) from a long public interview with the founder of a leading
naming agency — the firm credited with names such as Swiffer, BlackBerry, Pentium,
Sonos, Febreze, Azure, Impossible and Windsurf. Use these as principles, not scripture;
the anecdotes are there to make the principles memorable.

## Why the name deserves the effort

- **It is the most-used, longest-lived brand asset.** Nothing else in the brand gets used
  as often or for as long, and familiarity compounds every day it's in market. That
  makes getting it right early far cheaper than fixing it later.
- **Right, not just good.** The useful distinction isn't good vs bad but *right* vs
  merely good or okay. A right name creates an asymmetric advantage — most visibly in the
  first 90–120 days and the first year, when attention, retail interest and press are won.
- **Evidence from the field.** P&G wanted to call its new floor tool "Pro Mop" and
  couldn't have it legally. The agency noticed it wasn't really a mop, and that nobody
  enjoys mopping, so they made it fun: Swiffer, which grew into a multi-billion-dollar
  brand. A rival launched around the same time with a comfortable, mop-saying name and
  stayed a fraction of the size. Codium, a coding tool nobody could spell or search for,
  took off after becoming Windsurf.
- **The classic founder mistake:** the lawyer needs a name for incorporation papers, so
  the founders spend an afternoon on it — and later pay again, in time and money, to
  rename.

## The three jobs of a right name

1. **Get attention** — harder now than it has ever been.
2. **Hold attention** — be *processing-fluent*: pronounceable, with something inside
   the brain can grab and understand.
3. **Surprise** — not comfortable, not popular. This is the one most people get wrong,
   and it's why so many names are merely okay.

Right names are therefore **original in their category** (they don't have to be coined —
just new in this context), **linguistically fluent** (familiar yet surprising — the
agency's phrase is *surprisingly familiar*, because the lazy brain welcomes the easy
part and wakes up for the surprising part), and **unexpected** — relative to the company
as much as the category. Azure turned heads coming from Microsoft, whose portfolio was
mostly descriptive; the same word from a company already known for playful names
wouldn't have.

## The comfort trap

Picture two zones. In the **invisible zone**, names are highly familiar and barely
distinctive: lots of consensus, a "safe choice", and nothing remarkable. In the
**tension zone**, half the room dislikes the name; energy is high and opinions split.
Comfortable names do well in naive research because people can instantly say what the
product is — which is exactly why they disappear on the shelf.

Polarization is information. When Intel's engineers wanted a sensible "professional chip"
style name, Andy Grove backed Pentium precisely *because* it was polarizing: a name that
splits the room has energy in it. The agency now tells clients: if this divides your
organization, good — you may not choose it, but study what's creating that energy.

## Strategy before creativity

- **Look at the landscape first** and put everything there into a bucket marked "we're not
  going there". (We may come back, but we start knowing where everyone else already is.)
- **List everything the product has, then look hard at the people**: their needs and
  expectations, climbing the ladder to the *ultimate benefit*. For fiber: past gut health
  and regularity to *you feel lighter* — which is where a name like Feather comes from.
- **Ask the four questions** — How do you define winning? (Put six executives in a room
  and you'll get six answers.) What do you have that helps you win? What do you need to
  win? What do you need to say?
- **Write a creative framework** — deliberately a *window* people can walk through and
  create, rather than five rules to conform to.
- **When there's no magic-bullet feature** (the usual case), the name carries the
  ultimate benefit or an attitude. Impossible's founders wanted something earthy and
  natural that would fit in at a health-food store; the agency argued that fitting in was
  the problem and the name had to make a claim.

## The treasure hunt

- **Map the whole ocean, then dive.** (The gold-ship story: map everything, decide where
  the odds are, then dive.) Explore every territory before committing to one.
- **Quantity leads to quality.** Stuck clients typically show 50–100 names — the point
  where they stopped, and where everyone else stopped too. The agency works through
  around 2,000 per project. Most are "trash", and the trash is necessary.
- **Ask questions, not for names.** The founder uses AI tools to ask about the territory
  (aviation, what makes a plane lift, other words for light → sun → *sol*), then crafts
  names himself.
- **Build enriched databases.** Latin and Greek roots, Greek and Roman mythology, and a
  periodic table loaded with articles and lore (Pentium came out of that) — the riches are
  in the enrichment, not the bare list.
- **File by quality, not category.** Names made for a small car go into a "small things"
  bank next to names made for a small processor. Connecting seemingly irrelevant things —
  *synchronicity* — is, in the founder's view, the fundamental layer of creativity.
- **Two unfamiliar magazines.** Before finishing any creative piece, spend 30 minutes with
  publications you'd never normally read, looking for connections; roughly a third of the
  time it yields a new angle.

## How the teams work

- **No brainstorming sessions, no freelancers.** After 18 months of tracking where names
  actually came from, the agency found they came from individuals and two-person teams.
  Groups bring peer pressure and a slow cascade of evaluation; outsiders lack the
  context and the team spirit.
- **Three teams, three perspectives.** One knows everything about the product. One gets
  the product plus a twist (the fiber *also gives energy*), which changes their angle and
  their names. One isn't told about the product at all and names an analog (athletic
  performance) — three genuinely different lists.
- **Cycles.** The first cycle tests whether the assignments were right: did the twist
  yield anything? the analog? Redial what didn't work. After about two cycles, review
  against the creative framework and send the survivors to legal and linguists.
- **Everyone has permission to fail.** If nobody is failing, nobody is searching the
  whole ocean.

## Encourage, don't evaluate (while generating)

- Lead rather than manage, and *encourage* — the root of the word is courage. People
  only bring risky ideas to someone who won't slap them down.
- When a list arrives, **speculate with it rather than evaluate it**. Evaluation comes
  later and it's real — but it has its own time.
- **Coaching phrases.** "That's interesting." "I wouldn't have thought of that." When you
  feel the urge to say "too expensive" or "won't pass legal", say "I wish we could make
  that cheaper" or "how could we modify that word so it's legally available?" — you've
  handed over a problem instead of a verdict, and people love solving problems.
- **The approximate-thinking line.** Draw a line: bizarre/absurd/illegal ideas at one end,
  safe/workable at the other, *approximate thinking* in the middle. Let teams travel from
  bizarre toward approximate — and stop there to look at what they've got. Permission to
  be approximate makes creativity spike.
- **Separate the rooms** (Disney reputedly kept separate spaces for dreaming, planning and
  judging).

## Judging

- **AI makes generation cheap, so judging is the advantage**: separating right names
  from good and okay ones, using data rather than taste alone.
- **Tools of judgment:** processing fluency and memorability; consonant–vowel rhythm (the
  earliest pattern children produce — ma-ma, da-da); sound symbolism (some sounds read
  fast, some reliable — B tested as one of the most reliable sounds across languages);
  trademark screening by specialists; linguists in every target market.
- **Gut scores with no sevens** force a decision.

## Presenting

- **Proof of concept, not a list.** Show each name in context — a premium black-and-white
  ad, a headline, a shirt, the side of a bus — after first checking internally that it's
  *believable* in the first split second.
- **Supporting copy carries the category** ("Feather — the lightweight fiber you need";
  Swiffer didn't need to say mop because the line around it did).
- **Every famous name was rejected at first.** BlackBerry was dismissed in the first
  meeting. The agency took it off the table *for now* and brought it back with reasons: B
  is one of the most reliable sounds, *black* travels across languages, a berry is
  delightful — and the big competitors would never have the courage to put it on a
  device. That last point turned the room.
- **After the name:** descriptor and positioning line, a short story, nomenclature for
  what comes next, and light tracking research over the first 90–120 days.

## Calibration (the founder's quick gut takes, for flavour only)

SpaceX scored a 10 — the meaning is expected, but X reliably signals innovation.
HubSpot an 8 — memorable rhythm. Grok a 3 or 4 — an unpleasant sound. On X vs Twitter,
he'd have stuck with Twitter. Names he admires: DreamWorks, and Lexus for its structure
(X in the middle again). The best political slogan in his view: Reagan's "Morning in
America" — hope that wasn't false.
