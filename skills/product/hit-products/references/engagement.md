# Engagement, Retention and Distribution

How to make a product people come back to, and how to grow it, drawing on Zynga's
practice as described in *Life at the Speed of Play* and the Lenny's Podcast interview.
The mechanic descriptions are written fresh for this skill.

## 1. Start from the core loop

The **core loop** is the main action users repeat and the payoff that brings them back:
plow → plant → harvest; take a turn → wait for a friend → take a turn; post → get
reactions → post again. Every engagement idea should make the core loop more satisfying,
more frequent, or more social. When FarmVille entered an already proven category, the
team put its craft into the core loop first: better crop art and math, and fewer clicks
for the most repeated actions.

If the core loop isn't loved, no mechanic layered on top will save it. Mechanics
amplify a loop; they can't replace one.

## 2. Why people come back: motives

- **Invest, express, connect.** Zynga's shorthand for its games. Users put something
  in (time, care, creations) that makes the product more theirs. They express
  themselves and are seen as creative. Pincus's line is that people don't need to *be*
  creative, they want to *feel* creative, and tools like Midjourney deliver that. And
  they connect with people through it.
- **Permission to play.** Busy adults justify time on something if it feels social or
  productive. Social games beat "empty calories" by letting people keep up with friends.
  Ask what the product lets users tell themselves.
- **Make the virtual real.** Attaching real-world stakes (money, status, real
  relationships, real identity) makes people care far more.
- **A basic human urge.** Reid Hoffman's "seven deadly sins" filter: products that reach
  hundreds of millions tap an innate, slightly selfish drive. He maps Facebook to vanity,
  Twitter to wrath, LinkedIn to greed and Zynga to sloth. If you can't name the urge,
  mass adoption is unlikely.
- **Adrenaline versus empty calories.** Pincus distinguishes products people are excited
  to open from ones they consume like potato chips and later feel bad about. (He cites
  people becoming proud of quitting a social app.) Positive calories are what last.
- **Productivity.** Big social products also gave people *social productivity*: staying
  in the loop with hundreds of people cheaply. When products trade that for time-wasting
  engagement, they open the door for a successor.

## 3. Social design: the Cocktail Party

The Cocktail Party is the instinct behind most of the people-web. Everyone is looking for
the gathering that has:

- **Curated people** (some you know, some worth meeting),
- **the right context** and purpose, mixed with fun,
- **trust**, and
- good **leads**: dates, jobs, recommendations, deals, files, ideas. That's what turns
  an obligation ("I should show up") into wanting to be there.

Design questions:

- Where is the cocktail party already happening, or wanting to happen? Bring the
  product there (Zynga put games inside the social network where people were already
  hanging out), or host a better one.
- What are the leads? The better the signal-to-noise of leads, the more valuable the
  network. Friend-of-friend dating leads beat random ones; a network gated by a shared
  email domain gives instant trust.
- **Trust is non-negotiable.** Pincus attributes his social network's failure mainly to
  getting trust wrong (unwanted content and messages drove mainstream users away).
  Facebook won partly because real identity created extraordinary trust.
- **Reciprocity drives retention.** The strongest social loops need another person to
  respond: turn-based play, gifts returned, help returned. They create a gentle social
  obligation to come back.
- **Community over-delivers.** People are lonely. Ways to meet and gather nearly always
  beat expectations, and communities often outlast the product they formed on.

## 4. Metrics: invent a leading one, aim at day 365

**Invent your own leading metric.** DAU, retention and revenue are outcomes, and they
lag. Find a product-specific behavior that predicts long-term retention *and* that teams
can move week to week: hands played (poker), turns taken (word games), Facebook's early
"seven friends" threshold.

Zynga's **Active Social Network (ASN)** counted *round trips* with other people: you
take a turn and they take one back, you send a gift and get one back. Pincus reports
that going from zero to one ASN gave about an 80% chance of the user returning within
the next month. At around four, users were very likely to show up on most days of the
following month. A metric like that gives every team a clear thing to build toward.

**Avoid metrics teams can't move.** Zynga spent two quarters trying to raise its Net
Promoter Score directly and couldn't. When teams have a metric they can't move, they
ignore it and drift to the one they can (usually revenue), which leads to squeezing the
lemon.

**Beware local optimums.** A daily-chore feature (a bakery players had to "work" every
day for coins) raised short-term engagement and lowered long-term retention, because it
turned play into a job.

**Retention beats virality.** A product with strong invites and weak retention is a
**sinking speedboat**: it needs ever more speed (installs) or bailing (re-engagement)
just to stay afloat. Plug the hole: improve retention. Pincus's early social network had
invite open rates and acceptance rates most products would kill for, and still failed.

**Day 365 retention** (of the people using it today, how many used it on this day last
year) is the North Star for durability. Pincus believes Zynga was the only consumer
company tracking it. His rough estimates:

| Product type | D365 (his estimate) | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Utilities like Google, Amazon | ~80% | Eventually everyone uses it |
| Durable networks like LinkedIn, Snapchat | ~30% | A third of the world, potentially |
| Enduring game franchises (Words With Friends, Candy Crush, WoW) | ~10% | Enough for a multibillion-dollar "forever franchise" |
| Most games and novelty apps | ~0% | Fads |

You can't wait a year to measure it, but low D1 and D30 almost guarantee low D365. High
D30 with zero D365 is the most common fate. Users sense it too: when trying something new,
they ask themselves whether it's worth investing in. Ask of every product and feature:
*why would someone still be doing this in a year?*

Reference: around 60% DAU/MAU means a social product has become part of daily life; one
competitor's 30% DAU/MAU became Zynga's new internal benchmark.

## 5. The mechanics toolbox

Mechanics are interactions people enjoy repeating. Once a mechanic is proven it becomes a
reusable building block, and many of them work far beyond games (fitness, learning,
finance, retail, enterprise tools). Treat this list as Proven inventory to draw from, and
pick mechanics that strengthen *your* core loop.

**Two warnings.** Classic reward mechanics are so reliable that they can produce false
signal: they lift engagement on almost anything and can hide a weak core. And gamifying
doesn't mean bolting a random game onto a product (spin-the-wheel pop-ups everywhere are
the cautionary example). Some mechanics below, especially chance-based rewards and pay-to-
skip, carry ethical and regulatory weight, particularly for children or vulnerable users.
Use them deliberately and fairly, or not at all.

**Progress and mastery**
- Points, levels, experience, badges: visible progress for doing the core action.
- Unlocks: new content, abilities or areas that appear as the user advances.
- Quests and missions: guided goals that teach the product and introduce new features.
- Streaks and steady daily rewards: small, reliable payoffs for showing up each day
  (Duolingo, Snapchat streaks, activity rings).

**Collection and ownership**
- Sets to complete: the urge to finish a collection.
- Crafting: gather inputs and combine them into something more valuable; satisfying in
  any medium and long-lived as a loop.
- Decoration and personal space: a place users shape that expresses them.
- Objects that matter: turn static parts of the product into things that do something
  and lead somewhere deeper.

**Surprise and chance**
- Hidden extras (easter eggs) that reward curiosity and get shared.
- Random bonus rewards during normal use.
- Paid mystery boxes (handle with care).
- Limited-edition or time-limited items that create urgency.

**Social and reciprocal**
- Gifting things of real value to friends.
- Turn-based play: the most natural social obligation to return.
- Helping and hiring friends: pooled effort on a shared asset, where each person's
  progress depends on others.
- Teams, clubs and leagues: cooperative and competitive groups.
- Neighbors shown in the product: visible friends who use it too, with something to do
  together.
- Leaderboards against friends and everyone.
- Status markers: visible signs of achievement or standing.
- Communication channels, including at least one kept clean and high-value so users
  always check it.
- Social gates: some content unlocks only through interaction with others.

**Economy and pacing**
- Two currencies: one scarce and valuable (often tied to real money), one plentiful for
  experimentation and rewards.
- Energy or timers that pace play, with an option to wait or pay.
- Grind-or-pay: always offer a fun free path to a reward. Pincus's rule of thumb is that
  for every paying user, about 19 will jump through hoops to avoid paying, so make the
  hoops worthwhile for them and useful for you.
- Expansions and seasons: regular new worlds, chapters or content packs.
- Locked previews: show what's coming inside the product itself, where it markets
  itself to the people already there.
- No bad outcomes: everyone gets a good, better or best result.

**Real-world crossover**
- Gamified purchases, codes on physical products, rewards that carry over to the real
  world. Virtual goods cost almost nothing to make, which lets partners offer far more
  value per dollar (Zynga's promotions with 7-Eleven and American Express).

## 6. Bold Beats

A **Bold Beat** is a fast, isolated, killable experiment aimed at a big positive
disruption of the user experience. It sits between two failure modes: **mouse nuts**
(tiny measurable tweaks that don't matter) and **boiling the ocean** (huge projects that
never ship). It answers the question: what's the boldest thing we can put in front of
users in the next 30 days?

Properties:
- Quick to build: hacky is fine, even preferred. Build it "wrong" to see if it works.
- Isolated: it can be turned off without damaging the core product.
- Fast feedback: watch metrics closely right after launch (Zynga tracked revenue and
  engagement in five-minute intervals).
- Zynga's bar: **at least ~25% of users try it, and a key metric moves by ≥10%**. The
  real win was when users lit up social media about it (an "OMFG moment", in Bing
  Gordon's phrase).
- If it hits, **then** build it properly.

Examples, paraphrased:
- **Animals that move** (FarmVille): about half a day of work, a few alternating images.
  Players went wild and started imagining new dimensions (breeding horses), which opened
  years of new content.
- **A mystery crane across the river** (CityVille): one unexplained image set off
  community speculation; the later bridge unlocked a whole new waterfront area.
- **The lonely cow** (FarmVille): a feed story saying a friend's cow had wandered off and
  needed help getting home. It was silly and emotional, and brought roughly a million
  more players the next day. It became a pattern across games: re-engagement via
  **social breadcrumbs** that invite people back to help someone.
- **Expansion cities** (Mafia Wars): a travel menu listed possible new cities to see
  which got clicks. Chicago won, and expansions became quarterly events.
- **Duolingo Stories**: short character-driven narratives that made practice emotional.
- **Lost's hatch**: a single mystery that added a whole dimension and kept an audience
  for seasons.

Bold Beats work outside games and outside consumer: a fun-first debit card, a gamified
retail promotion, even compliance training with a leaderboard. Pincus's view is that
the more boring the category, the more room there is to delight.

Make Bold Beats a standing quota (for example, every team ships at least one per
quarter, written into its goals). Teams that are either crushing it or in crisis
otherwise never try anything new (the Penguin Effect; see `roadmap.md`).

### Forever franchises

For a product people already love, improving it is often worth more than launching
something new. Zynga's turnaround rested on the economics of **forever franchises**:
long-lived products run as live services, with steady Bold Beats, seasons and
expansions. They were judged on loyalty (a D365 around 10% or better) rather than launch
spikes. The same turnaround showed what happens when a team swaps a loved franchise's
proven core for an all-new version, or bets on expensive new launches instead of the
franchise. Protect the core, and innovate at the edges in isolation.

## 7. Golden Mechanics

When a Bold Beat clearly works, promote it to a **Golden Mechanic**: a proven building
block every future product and team reuses. Zynga's included gifting, crafting,
expansions, neighbors, helping and hiring friends, and turn-based social play. Keep a
written playbook of your own Golden Mechanics with the evidence for each. It's your
private Proven inventory, and nobody objects to copying their own team.

## 8. Distribution is part of the product

"Build it and they will come" is a hope strategy. Design distribution in from day one:

- **Go where people already are.** Casual games on a social network were like comics in
  the newspaper: nobody goes to a comic shop, but people read the strip they trip over
  every morning. Put the product in the daily path.
- **Hack an existing platform's distribution.** Many big consumer services started on
  someone else's surface (YouTube embeds on MySpace; Zynga on Facebook's newly opened
  platform and feed). Pincus also points to under-used channels: big mature platforms
  everyone has stopped trying, and optimizing for AI answer engines (generative engine
  optimization).
- **Make usage do the inviting**: turn-based requests, help requests, shareable
  artifacts (tbh's screenshot-worthy poll results), reciprocity, an imported graph so
  the product isn't empty on day one.
- **Breadcrumbs and reminders that people welcome**: social, emotional, useful. Not
  spam. Being too viral can make non-users resent you.
- **Buy mindshare early on new platforms**, when attention is cheap.
- **When consumer distribution is broken** (app discovery is close to dead; Pincus notes
  average new app installs per user per month are near zero), start with prosumers and
  power users who care enough to find the product and pay for it.
- **Use marketing as a product test**: locked teasers and ad variants tell you what has
  heat before you build.
- **Platform risk is real.** Building on someone else's platform gives distribution but
  makes you a guest who can be cut off. Zynga lost about a third of its traffic quickly
  when its host changed the rules. Keep control where you can.

### Cold start

If the product is empty without other people or content (ratings, listings, a social
graph), plan for the empty room:

- **Win the smallest dense unit first**: one campus, one team, one neighborhood, one
  event. Facebook went school by school. A network gated by a shared email domain gives
  instant trust and density.
- **Be worth opening before the network exists**: some single-player value (menus,
  tools, personal tracking), an imported graph, or content the founders seed by hand.
- **Make the empty state do work.** FarmVille's empty friends bar looked like friction,
  but it drove invitations.
- **Use structure to raise density**: content that repeats (dishes, venues, routes) can
  build up ratings over time rather than resetting daily.

## 9. Anti-patterns

- **Mouse nuts**: many small, measurable features that move nothing and slowly make the
  product worse.
- **Squeezing the lemon**: optimizing monetization with pop-ups, harder-to-find close
  buttons and longer unskippable ads, instead of fixing complaints or adding bold value.
- **Chores disguised as engagement**: daily obligations that aren't fun.
- **Changing what users rely on** without testing (Poker 2.0's revolt).
- **High-fidelity traps**: expensive rebuilds (Pincus singles out 3D in games) that are
  slow, so you can't find the fun quickly.
- **Novelty without retention**: a viral spike from a mechanic that fades.
- **Metrics nobody can move**, which teams quietly ignore.

## 10. Engagement audit checklist

0. **Diagnose before building.** Where exactly do users drop, and is each drop
   something engagement can fix? Paywalls, trial endings, pricing, seasonality and a
   changing acquisition mix all look like engagement problems in a retention curve.
   Then check the category's **proven basics** are in place (onboarding best practice,
   trial and paywall hygiene, notification patterns). Even Sid Meier's social game
   failed on a clumsy first-time experience. Fix those failures before inventing.
1. What is the core loop, and is it loved on its own? (Talk to or watch real users.)
2. What leading metric predicts retention here? Is it product-specific and movable? If
   not, propose one (a round-trip or depth-of-investment measure).
3. D1 / D7 / D30 numbers and the D365 story: why would someone be here in a year?
4. Which motives does it serve (invest, express, connect, permission, real stakes, a
   basic urge)? Which are missing?
5. Social structure: is there reciprocity? Trust? Good leads?
6. Which proven mechanics would strengthen the core loop? Which existing ones are just
   lemon-squeezing?
7. A Bold Beats backlog: 3–5 ideas, each with the hack version, isolation, the target
   (≥25% try, ≥10% lift on a named metric) and the kill rule.
8. Distribution: what in normal use brings in the next user?
9. Close with: *what will our users thank us for?* Rank the backlog by that.
