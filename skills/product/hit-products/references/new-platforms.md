# New Platforms: Building Where Nothing Is Proven Yet

Pincus calls emerging platforms "new planets": AI, robotics, space, crypto, and the
blurring of virtual and real. They break Proven Better New because nothing is proven
for this audience on this platform yet. Don't throw the framework away. Adapt it.

## 1. Two waves

Every platform shift has a **first wave** everyone predicts: the obvious port of
existing businesses (e-commerce replacing catalogs; online versions of offline
institutions). These are often overfunded before they make sense. Then comes a
**second wave** that nobody can imagine until they've lived on the planet for a while:
eBay and Craigslist on the early web; Uber and Airbnb in the social/mobile era, which
depended on a new kind of trust between strangers.

Second waves often follow the **Jevons paradox**: when something becomes nearly free
(listing an item, publishing, a ride request, now intelligence via tokens), demand
explodes in ways nobody predicted. Ask: what is becoming nearly free, and what will
people suddenly do far more of?

## 2. Fuzzy Proven

When nothing is proven on the new platform, find the **atomic unit** of proven
*behavior* and make it native.

- Get to the right level of abstraction. "Taxis" was the wrong proven unit (it leads to
  booking a cab by text). "A paid ride from A to B" was the right one, and it left room
  for a phone-native experience and a radical New (anyone can drive).
- Keep the proven nuance while adapting. Many teams treated mobile as a license to
  reinvent everything and failed. Supercell kept FarmVille's proven elements and
  changed only what touch and mobile sessions demanded.
- Look for the proven behavior hiding in another context, often another country or
  platform. Pincus suggests a Chinese companion chatbot with a huge user base was the
  first real proven signal for conversational AI as a consumer product, years before
  ChatGPT.
- Expect fuzzy-proven attempts to fail more often. Their value is also that they make
  the team native fast. (Pincus's own quick AI chat app failed before launch because
  the proven competitor's answers were simply better than his team's. That was a
  lesson in what was actually proven.)

## 3. Get native

You can't design the second wave from outside. Live on the planet before it's worth it:

- **Use the technology daily** for real tasks, even when it's clumsy. Reid Hoffman
  learned more in five minutes of driving an electric car than from understanding
  the idea intellectually.
- **Be a hobbyist and a hacker.** Early hacky versions (a crash-prone first product, a
  barely useful early code assistant) show people the future and often lead to owning
  the market. Engineers tend to dismiss small early innovations because they're easy to
  build. Then nobody builds them.
- **Endure the friction on purpose.** Each painful workaround builds muscle memory, so
  you'll be first to recognize when someone finally solves it.
- **Make the team native.** For example, the Shopify CEO's rule that teams must show why
  AI can't do the work before asking for more headcount.

## 4. Permission to be too early

You'll almost never time a shift perfectly, so being too early is the better mistake.
Being early has advantages: less competition, forgiving early users, hobbyist
communities hungry for new things, room to experiment cheaply. Give yourself permission
to "waste" time on small, narrow, interesting products. Established companies should
consider reserving a fixed share of resources for the next platform each year, even
though it trades dollars for pennies at first. (Zynga's real mobile mistake, by Pincus's
account, wasn't missing it. It was not committing hard enough, including not buying the
mobile leader when it could.)

## 5. Curious edges and surface area

- **Curious edges**: where the user's own life meets an emerging technology, often
  disguised as a personal problem (a family member's rare condition and gene editing; a
  spouse's illness that became a precision-medicine company). Genuine motivation keeps
  you exploring when it's hard and doesn't yet pay.
- **Increase surface area**: get out of the office and meet people already building on
  the planet. A chance demo (a writer's AI voice bot that phones people to collect their
  stories) can open a new instinct vein (voice as an interface).

## 6. True signal still means behavior

New platforms produce a lot of false signal. Pincus's case is Web3: billions invested,
little consumer traction beyond trading. Games were the expected first use case, but
nobody plays a game because it's on a blockchain. That might help monetization, but it
doesn't make the game fun. Watch out for "drinking the Kool-Aid" evidence (a startup
whose main customer is itself). True signal looks like millions of people doing the
thing within months, and founders scrambling for more servers.

## 7. Three pioneer styles

From Pincus's interviews:

- **Reid Hoffman: people, people, people.** Everything starts from what people care
  about most, which is other people. He builds networks years ahead so they bring back
  signal, filters ideas by the basic human urge they serve, and prefers bits to atoms for
  speed of iteration (except for deliberate impact bets).
- **Cyan Banister: pursue the weird.** She schedules unproductive time, follows odd
  coincidences, funds ideas others call insane or mistimed, and gets native by doing
  (she tried manufacturing a simple product herself to understand the supply gap).
- **Neal Stephenson: find your Comic-Con.** Find the community already living a
  primitive version of the future, spending money and vacation time on it. Build
  infrastructure that lets many people experiment, rather than predicting the one right
  answer. Ask: what do you believe that others don't?

## 8. Instincts Pincus is chasing in AI (raw material, not proven)

Offer these as instinct prompts when the user is exploring AI products. Label them as
one founder's hunches, not evidence. Each still needs Proven-Better-New thinking and
cheap tests.

- **Make the AI cocktail party rowdy.** People now spend hours with AI assistants, but
  alone. It's a quiet, lonely party, like the web before social networks. Who makes it
  social, and socially productive?
- **Agents as social brokers.** An agent that knows both people's context could broker
  plans, introductions, dating and job leads while revealing less than it knows. Think
  of a trust membrane that adjusts how much attention to ask of each person, handling
  the awkward asymmetries (one person keen, the other busy).
- **A social productivity step-function.** Early social networks made keeping up with
  hundreds of people cheap. Agents could do that again, without wasting people's time.
- **Services that never made economic sense**, now viable at near-zero marginal cost:
  his favorite example is a 24/7 personal travel agent that knows your context and
  rebooks you mid-trip when things go wrong.
- **Design for free tokens** (an idea he credits to Garry Tan): imagine today's token
  costs as essentially free within a year or two, and design consumer products for that
  world. Unlike a dot-com business selling goods at a loss, the cost curve is actually
  falling.
- **Distribution is the open question.** In Pincus's view (at the time of the
  interview), AI chat isn't yet a true platform for third-party apps, and app discovery
  is close to dead, so consumer AI launches face terrible odds. That favors prosumer and
  power-user wedges, and baking distribution into the product.

### Open veins Pincus points to from earlier eras

Also from the book, as instinct prompts:

- **Online dating is always broken.** Every generation tolerates bad leads. Curated and
  trust-gated networks (human-curated apps, campus-only matching) keep finding room.
- **A Napster for live music.** Fans record every show and the recordings go nowhere.
  A cocktail party for each fan community is still missing.
- **Gamified commerce.** Codes on products, fun-first cards and virtual rewards cost
  almost nothing to produce, and most retailers still haven't tried them.

**Experiment now.** Pincus's warning on the podcast: while distribution is broken,
experiments are cheap. Once a channel opens, a flood of competitors makes the same test
far more expensive.

## 9. What to do this week

- Pick one curious edge and use the new technology on it daily.
- Meet three people already building on the planet.
- Ship one fuzzy-proven experiment in days: proven behavior, native form, isolated New.
- Write down what you believe that others don't, and one cheap way to test it.
