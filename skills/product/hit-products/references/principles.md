# Principles

The mindset behind every phase. These ideas come from Mark Pincus's *Life at the Speed
of Play* and his Lenny's Podcast interview; the wording and structure here are a
distillation, not his text.

## 1. Instincts versus ideas

An **instinct** is a gut-level feeling about people: something is missing, too hard, or
could be far better. An **idea** is one particular way of acting on it.

When people see a hit and say "I had that idea," they usually had the instinct. Plenty
of people sensed that phones and computers would merge; the iPhone was one specific,
very hard-to-get-right idea. Pincus sensed in the early 2000s that hailing a cab
should be as easy as texting, and registered a domain for booking taxis by SMS. The
instinct was right. The idea, ordering a conventional taxi, wasn't. Uber became
possible years later when GPS phones, maps and trust in strangers on the internet made a
different idea work.

His rule of thumb: a seasoned founder's instincts are right around 95% of the time;
their ideas are right maybe 25% of the time, often less. If you believe that, you
behave differently:

- You expect the current idea to be wrong and plan the next three.
- You stay emotionally attached to the instinct, not to the implementation.
- You read a failed idea as information about the idea, not a verdict on the instinct.
- You notice when someone else ships the right idea on *your* instinct. That is the
  strongest validation you'll ever get. Switch, copy, or invest; don't compete with it
  out of pride.

Founders often find the winning idea only once money is about to run out, because that
is when they finally kill ideas quickly. The goal is to act that way before it's forced
on you.

## 2. Instinct veins

Some instincts are deep enough to mine for a career. Pincus names two of his:

- **"It just works"**: technology is too hard for normal people; remove the friction.
  It produced an offline-browsing product, help-desk automation software, and (for his
  cofounder) a spam-filtering company.
- **The Cocktail Party**: everyone is looking for the gathering with the right people,
  the right context, fun with purpose and trust, where the best "leads" come from
  (dates, jobs, ideas, deals). It produced an early social network, Zynga's social games,
  and early investments in Facebook and Twitter.

Ask the user what themes keep coming back for them. A vein that recurs across years and
projects is a stronger foundation than a clever idea from last week.

The book also notes that deep instincts often form in the **Abyss**: the open,
unstructured stretch between projects. It is uncomfortable but useful. That is where
people sample widely, notice patterns, and calibrate taste. Don't rush someone out of it
into the first available B+ idea.

## 3. The body of water

Pick the market before the boat. In the right body of water most boats float; in the
wrong one, even a great boat sits on a dry lake bed. (Pincus's example: in 1995, "retail
software" looked like the big market and "free internet services" looked tiny; within a
decade the first had shrunk and the second had become too big to call one industry.)

Four quick tests:

1. **Room to enter.** Can a newcomer get noticed, or is it so crowded and hot that
   standing out is impossible?
2. **A proven way to make money.** Is there a clear line of sight to revenue and
   sustainable margins in this market?
3. **Room to innovate.** Favorite pattern: a mature market that is quietly a growth
   market waiting for someone to innovate on its core value (search before Google,
   games before social games).
4. **Long-term tailwinds.** Does it ride macro trends the founder believes in?

The right water is often contrarian. It isn't what's fashionable to fund this quarter.

## 4. True signal, false signal, hope

**True signal** is unmistakable. Everything comes back positive at once:

- The maker can't stop using it, despite the flaws.
- Friends and target users love it without being asked to be polite, and show it to
  others.
- People do the core action again and again, and come back without being reminded.
- Growth is organic, and the problem is keeping up (servers, supply, support).
- Benchmarks: for a social product, around 60% of monthly users active on a given day is
  a sign it has become part of daily life. Early Facebook reportedly took a fifth of a
  campus in week one and most of the rest in week two.
- Depth beats breadth early. A product that 20 people truly love is a stronger signal
  than one a million people find okay (a point Pincus credits to Emmett Shear). Look for
  the small group that would be genuinely upset if it disappeared.

**False signals** feel encouraging but don't predict a hit:

- Investors, big partners or press are interested. (Smart money is not user signal.)
- Novelty virality without retention: invites get opened, people try it once, and the
  boat is sinking faster than you can fill it.
- "Viable": people *can* use it, so it might work.
- Positive feedback on things that were copied from a proven product, while the new
  part is weak.
- Engagement bought with mechanics (rewards, streaks, gambling-style loops) that would
  lift any product and hide whether the core is loved.
- Your own team using it because they have to.

**Hope** is confidence with no basis in what users have done. **Belief** rests on
evidence from real behavior. **Conviction** is belief strong enough to bet heavily on.
*Kill hope before hope kills you*: every plan should replace hope with a test. Pincus
describes the best makers as collecting winnings rather than placing bets. By launch day
they already know they have a hit, because they built the evidence first.

## 5. B+ is the enemy of A

A B+ idea is the most dangerous kind. It's good enough to raise money, win some
customers, hire people and get a little traction. It is never good enough to become
great. A clearly bad idea dies quickly; a B+ can absorb years.

Tests for intellectual honesty:

- **If you're asking whether it's an A, it isn't.** With the right one, the way with the
  right partner, you don't wonder.
- Would your smartest friends put their own money in?
- Are you pursuing it out of desperation, for example to escape the Abyss?
- Are you torn between two directions? Then often neither is right.
- Does the excitement come from users or from you?

Being truly ambitious means: ruthless objectivity about ideas, willingness to kill them
often, no attachment to a specific implementation, and testing several variants instead
of going all in on one. When something is graded B+, the honest next step is one of:
use it to learn (what part had heat?), look for something nearby that's proven, or stop
and start the next shot. Pincus says that killing his own long-running project after
four years brought the most creative stretch he'd had in years.

## 6. Start smaller than feels dignified

The more ambitious you are, the humbler the starting point should be. Big hits usually
start embarrassingly small: a poker game on Facebook, a site for one campus, an internal
chat tool at a failing game studio. Pincus's failed social network tried to be social,
professional, groups and classifieds at once. It was wide and shallow, and it lost to
narrower products. His comeback, Zynga, started with one engineer and one deliberately
unambitious game.

Successful repeat founders are especially at risk. Money and recruiting come easily, so
they can chase a big vision before finding product-market fit. A newcomer who starts
narrow can have the advantage. Small companies can follow flaky threads that giants
can't justify, and those threads are often where big ideas come from.

## 7. Taste and staying close to the metal

Great product makers have a point of view, the way a good chef has opinions about their
own cooking. Taste is built through:

1. Deep immersion in the category (use everything, including the failures).
2. Constant experimentation and refinement.
3. Regular contact with user reactions.
4. Holding high standards when it's inconvenient.

Taste can also mislead. The maker's idea of magic may not match what users think about
at the moment they need the product. Airbnb's team called one version of this the
"Pincus Problem": he pointed out that celebrity-hosted experiences were delightful, but
not what a typical traveler wants when planning a surf trip. Check the magic against the
user's real moment of use.

Treat your own reactions as data. If you lose interest in something you thought would be
huge, notice it. If you keep using something despite its flaws, notice that too. A
**focus group of one** (the maker, or one person who perfectly represents the audience)
often gives clearer signal than a survey. Tell them a short story of the product and
watch for adrenaline: do they start imagining what happens next? Then check, quickly and
for real, that your taste generalizes to millions.

**Staying close to the metal** means the leader stays in the details: the product, the
users, the code, the data. People who are in the trenches are closest to the right
answer. When decisions drift away from them, culture turns cynical and products lose
their emotional center.

**You don't have to be first.** Zynga's mantra was best to market, not first to market.
A sharp number two that learns from the leader's mistakes can win. Pincus's first
company was a distant number two in its category and still sold for a good price, while
the hyped leader ended at zero.

## 8. Being right beats scale

The first job of a founder is to be right about the product. Scale is a consequence of
quality, not the goal. A company can do many things wrong with the right product, and
almost nothing saves the wrong one. (Pincus borrows the pilot's order of priorities:
aviate, navigate, communicate. Get the product flying before you worry about investors,
press or org charts.)

The question that brought Zynga back during its turnaround: **"What will our players
thank us for?"** Use it on every roadmap, review and feature list. It cuts through
revenue squeezing, vanity metrics and internal politics.

## 9. Ambition, defined through the user's eyes

"Burn your résumé." If you define ambition by your peers' respect, you'll avoid copying,
chase novelty and build for awards. If you define it by what your actual users love, you
take good ideas wherever they come from and make the experience one inch better for
them. The best version of this goes unnoticed. Users just feel the product is right.

The ultimate target is an **Internet Treasure**: a service people can't remember life
before and can't imagine life without. It is rare, and it is reached through many cheap
shots on goal, not one heroic bet.
